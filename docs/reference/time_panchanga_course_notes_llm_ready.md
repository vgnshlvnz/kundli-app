## 1. Time in Hindu tradition

- The Hindu concept of time is Kala. It is based on astronomy: tracking planetary motions and celestial positions to measure everything from daily rituals to cosmic cycles.
- The Hindu calendar, the Panchanga, uses the positions of the Sun, Moon and other bodies to determine time.
- The ancient Vedic texts describe four measures of time — savana, solar, lunar and sidereal — and 27 constellations defined by taras (stars). The idea of 28 constellations and the movement of astronomical bodies already appears in the Atharvaveda (about 1000 BCE).

### 1.1 Key components of Hindu timekeeping

1. Cosmic time cycles (Kala Chakra):
   - Hindu cosmology views time as cyclical, not linear.
   - The major cycles are the Yugas (Satya, Treta, Dvapara, Kali), Manvantaras and Kalpas: vast spans describing creation, preservation and dissolution.
   - They express the rhythm of the universe and the repeated rise and fall of dharma.
2. Planetary motions:
   - The Sun, Moon and planets are tracked for position and movement.
   - Their motions determine auspicious timings (muhurta), eclipses and transits, which influence daily rituals and larger life events.
3. Panchanga:
   - The practical tool for timekeeping, combining solar and lunar calculations.
   - Its five limbs are Vara, Tithi, Nakshatra, Yoga and Karana.
   - These determine auspicious timings for rituals, festivals and daily practices.

### 1.2 Four measures of time

- The Surya Siddhanta takes the day as the starting point for measuring time.
- The civil (savana) day is the natural day: in India it is counted from sunrise to sunrise and is of variable length. It is an important element in all computations of time.
- One month is 30 savana days; one year is 360 days.

| No. | Measure | Basis | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Savana (civil day) | Sunrise to sunrise | The Surya Siddhanta takes the day as the starting point for measuring time |
| 2 | Saura (solar) | The Sun's motion through the zodiac | Defines months and years by solar transit |
| 3 | Chandra (lunar) | The Moon's phases and motion | Determines tithis (lunar days), festivals and many ritual timings |
| 4 | Nakshatra (sidereal) | The 27 nakshatras | Each constellation marks a distinct phase of the Moon's journey, with its own qualities and influences |

## 2. Philosophical foundations — cyclic time

- Kala (time; also Samay) is eternal, cyclic, and inseparable from creation and dissolution.
- Time is anadi (without beginning) and ananta (without end): it flows endlessly.
- Time is the backdrop of the three cosmic functions: Srishti (creation), Sthiti (preservation) and Laya (dissolution).
- Hindu thought emphasises cyclicality over linearity: the universe is born, sustained, dissolved and reborn in endless rhythm.
- Time is both measurable (calendars, yugas, Panchanga) and philosophical (a principle of cosmic order).

## 3. Cosmic time cycles (Kala Chakra)

### 3.1 Brahma and the Kalpa
- Time immemorial is measured in cycles called Kalpas. After each Kalpa there is another.
- A daytime of Brahma, the Lord of Creation, lasts one Kalpa, and his night another.
- By day the universe runs; by night everything dissolves, nothing is left, and Vishnu sleeps on Shesha Naga.

### 3.2 The four Yugas (Chaturyuga)
- Time moves in repeating cycles called Yugas, each showing a gradual decline in dharma (righteousness) and human values.
- The four Yugas together form a Mahayuga, which repeats endlessly. History is thus a cycle of ages of truth, balance, conflict and decline, not a straight line.
- The year of the gods, the divine year, is used only to describe these immense periods.
- Each Manvantara is divided into 71 Chaturyugas; each Chaturyuga has four Yugas.

| Yuga | Age | Duration in divine years | Duration in solar years | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- Mahayuga: the complete cycle of four Yugas = 4.32 million human years.
- Each Yuga reflects the moral and spiritual condition of humanity: the cosmic rhythm of rise and decline.

### 3.4 Manvantara
A Manvantara is the middle tier between a single Yuga cycle and a full Kalpa: the reign of a specific Manu, progenitor and lawgiver of humanity for that era.

- A Manvantara is the Age of Manu, the progenitor of mankind. The word combines Manu and antara: the duration, or lifespan, of a Manu.
- Each Manvantara is created and ruled by a specific Manu, who is himself created by Brahma.
- Each has its own Manu, seven sages (Saptarishis), a particular Indra, specialised deities and an avatar of Vishnu.
- Manu creates the world and all its species in that period. A Manvantara lasts the lifetime of a Manu; on his death Brahma creates another Manu to continue creation (Srishti), Vishnu takes a new avatar, and a new Indra and Saptarishis are appointed.
- 14 Manus and their Manvantaras make one Kalpa, or Day of Brahma.
- At the end of each Kalpa comes a period of dissolution (Pralaya) in which the world is destroyed and lies at rest: the Night of Brahma.

Components of a Manvantara (each perishes and is created anew at the end of the period):
- Manu: the progenitor of the era.
- Saptarishis: seven great sages who preserve knowledge.
  - First Manvantara: Marichi, Atri, Angiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, Vashishtha.
  - Current, 7th Manvantara (Vaivasvata): Atri, Vashishtha, Kashyapa, Gautama, Bharadvaja, Jamadagni, Vishvamitra.
- Indra.
- Devas.

### 3.5 Kalpa — Brahma's day and night
- 1 Kalpa = one full Day of Brahma = 4.32 billion years = 14 Manvantaras.
- 1 Manvantara = 71 Chaturyugas.
- The Night of Brahma (Pralaya) equals the Day in length.

Endings happen at different scales:
- The end of Kali Yuga is a reset within a Chaturyuga.
- The end of a Manvantara is a larger reset after 71 Chaturyugas.
- The end of a Kalpa is the grand dissolution, after 14 Manvantaras.

After the Night of Brahma the cycles repeat endlessly: Yugas within Manvantaras, Manvantaras within Kalpas, and Kalpas within Brahma's lifespan.

## 4. Timekeeping from planetary motions

Hierarchy: Day → Masa (month) → Ritu (season) → Ayana (half-year) → Samvatsara (year).

### 4.1 The Vedic day, sunrise and sunset
- A full day is Ahoratra: aha (day) + ratra (night).
- The cycle begins at sunrise, the start of Ahna (the day). The Sun is the visible form of time; its rising brings light, activity and the start of human duties and daily karma.
- Sunset begins Ratri (the night): a time for rest, reflection, withdrawal and inward practices.
- This daily rhythm is the basis of rituals such as Sandhya Vandana (morning and evening prayers) and the anchor for Panchanga calculations.

- Rising time: the first appearance of the Sun's upper limb (edge) above the horizon.
- Setting time: complete invisibility of the Sun's upper limb.
- In Hindu timekeeping, sunrise is taken as the moment the middle of the Sun's disc rises above the eastern horizon, and sunset as the moment the middle of the disc sets below the western horizon.
- Sunrise and sunset times depend on location and season.

### 4.3 Hora

- Hora is a division of time. The word comes from Ahoratra, dropping the first and last syllables.
- A hora influences daily activities according to its ruling planet.
- Each day has 24 horas, each about 1/24 of the day: 12 in the daytime (Ahas) and 12 at night (Ratri).
- Horas are counted from sunrise to sunrise, so their length varies with sunrise and sunset: about one hour, not exactly 60 minutes.
- Each hora has a planetary lord. The Sun, king of the solar system, rules the first day and the first hour of the first day.
- The lords follow the order Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon: from the slowest-moving to the fastest-moving planet as seen from the Earth. After the Moon the order returns to Saturn.

Calculation of the Hora Chakra:
1. Obtain the sunrise and sunset times for the location.
2. Determine the day and night durations.
3. Divide each of the day and night durations by 12.
4. Compute the hora table.

Worked example of the hora length:
1. Sunrise 6:07 AM, sunset 6:20 PM (18:20 in 24-hour format).
2. Day duration = 18:20 − 6:07 = 12 hours 13 minutes = 733 minutes.
3. 733 ÷ 12 = 61.0833 minutes.
4. 61 minutes = 1 hour 1 minute; 0.0833 minute × 60 = 5 seconds.
5. One day hora = 1 hour 1 minute 5 seconds.

### 4.6 Ayana
- One year consists of two Ayanas of six months each.
- An Ayana is the passage of the Sun to the north or the south of the equator.

## 5. Hindu calendars

- Southern India: months and year are solar and the day is civil. In practice each month and year is counted from sunrise.
- Northern India: the year is lunisolar. The month is lunar, divided into both lunar and civil days. The year has twelve or thirteen months, always beginning with the lunar month whose start next precedes the true start of the sidereal year.

### 5.1 Solar months (Sauramasa)
- Determined by the Sun's transit into each sign (rasi).
- The Sun's entry into a sign is called Sankranti or Sankramana.
- A solar year is the time the Sun takes to move 360°; a solar month is the time it takes to move 30°.
- Each solar month has 30 days, one day standing for exactly 1° of the Sun's motion.
- A month begins when the Sun enters a new sign; for example Mesha Masa begins when the Sun enters Aries.
- There are 12 solar months, beginning with Mesha.

Tamil solar months: the new year begins about 13 or 14 April, when the Sun enters Aries. The Sun stays a month in each sign, entering the next in the middle of the following calendar month, and transits all 12 signs in a year.

### 5.2 Lunar months (Chandramasa)
- Determined by the Moon's phases: two pakshas make one month.
- A new lunar month starts whenever the Sun and Moon are at the same longitude. The Moon then moves ahead and catches the Sun again after about 29–30 days.
- The name of the month is decided by the sign in which the Sun–Moon conjunction takes place. The names themselves come from the nakshatra the Moon is most likely to occupy at full Moon (Purnima, the 15th tithi). Example: after a conjunction in Pisces the full Moon is likely to fall in Chitra (23°20' Virgo to 6°40' Libra), so the month is Chaitra.
- There are 12 lunar months, beginning with Chaitra.
- Use: followed widely in North India and for most Hindu festivals; used in the Panchanga for tithi, nakshatra, yoga and karana.

Adhika Masa (extra month):
- A solar year has about 365.2425 days; a lunar year only about 355.
- About once every 3 years the difference accumulates to a month, and an extra lunar month occurs: the Sun–Moon conjunction falls twice in the same sign.
- Of the two months of the same name, one is Nija ("real") and the other Adhika ("extra").
- Example: conjunctions occurred at 0°23' Taurus on 15 May 1999 (5:35:32 pm IST) and again at 28°29' Taurus on 14 June 1999 (12:33:27 am IST). A conjunction in Taurus starts Jyeshtha, so 1999 had a Nija Jyeshtha and an Adhika Jyeshtha.
- The Adhika Masa keeps lunar years synchronised with solar years.

### 5.3 The 60-year cycle (Samvatsara Chakra)
- Samvatsara is a Sanskrit term for a full year.
- Varahamihira's Brihat Samhita details the 60-year cycle, the Brihaspati Chakra. Each year has a unique name, from Prabhava to Kshaya, after which the cycle repeats. Almanacs and astrology use these names to mark years.
- The 60 Samvatsaras form 3 groups of 20: Prabhava to Vyaya are assigned to Brahma, Sarvajith to Parabhava to Vishnu, and the remaining twenty to Shiva.
- The cycle is defined by the "Jovian year", tracked through Jupiter (Brihaspati) and Saturn (Shani).
- Jupiter takes about 12 years to circle the zodiac and Saturn about 30; their least common multiple is 60 years: 5 Jupiter cycles or 2 Saturn cycles.
- In those 60 years Jupiter and Saturn conjoin every 20 years, each meeting about 120° further along the zodiac.

## 6. Panchanga — the quality of time

- The quality of a day is based on the five elements (Pancha Tattva); the system is called Panchanga: pancha (five) + anga (limbs).
- The Vedic day starts at local sunrise, and the five limbs are determined at sunrise for the day.
- Panchanga calculations are based on the longitudes of the Sun and Moon, the fundamental sources of light and knowledge.

| Limb | Element | Planet indicating the quality of the element | Ruler stated in the limb's own section | Calculation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vara (Vasara, weekday) | Agni (fire, energy) | Mars | Surya (Sun) | From sunrise to the next sunrise |
| Tithi (with paksha) | Jala (water, fluid) | Venus | Venus | (Moon's longitude − Sun's longitude) ÷ 12° |
| Nakshatra | Vayu (air) | Saturn | Shani (Saturn) | Longitude of the Moon; 13°20' per nakshatra, divided into 4 padas |
| Karana | Prithvi (earth, solid) | Mercury | Budha (Mercury) | Half a tithi: 6° of Moon–Sun separation |
| Yoga | Akasha (ether, magnetic field) | Jupiter | Guru (Jupiter) | (Sun's longitude + Moon's longitude) ÷ 13°20' |

### 6.1 Vara (Vasara)
- "Vara" is from Vasara: vas means abode or residence, and ra means Agni.
- The Sun is the giver of life and rules over the Vara. The Sun's energy is released through the lord of the weekday (Vara lord).
- The placement of the Vara lord in a chart gives details of a person's basic level of health and general disposition in life. The Vara lord shows the ability to sustain life on earth.
- The twelve Sun signs are the twelve Adityas: the Sun in twelve forms.
- Sankranti, the day the Sun moves from one sign to another, is a transitional stage in which the old form is shed and a new one takes shape.
- Agni Dosha (fire flaw) occurs on Sankranti: the signs owned by the Vara lord lose their ability to create or sustain life.

### 6.2 Nakshatra
- The nakshatra is defined by the longitude of the Moon, which moves roughly 13°20' a day; each nakshatra is further divided into 4 padas.
- Its placement reveals how the orientation of the mind influences prana, shaping vitality and longevity.

### 6.3 Tithi

The Moon and its phases:
- The Moon produces no light of its own; it reflects sunlight. As it orbits the Earth the angle between Sun, Earth and Moon changes, altering how much of its lit half is visible.
- The Moon is tidally locked: the same side always faces the Earth. The phases are simply the fraction of the lit half that can be seen.
- Full Moon: the Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon, so the whole near side is lit.
- New Moon: the Moon lies between the Earth and the Sun, so the near side is dark.
- After the full Moon the visible lit fraction decreases (waning) until the new Moon; after the new Moon it increases (waxing) until the full Moon.
- One full cycle of phases, new Moon to new Moon, averages 29.53059 days.

Pakshas:
- Shukla Paksha (bright fortnight): the 15 days from the day after the new Moon (Amavasya) up to and including the full Moon. The Moon is waxing and is 0°–180° ahead of the Sun.
- Krishna Paksha (dark fortnight): the 15 days from the day after the full Moon (Purnima) to the new Moon. The Moon is waning and is 180°–360° ahead of the Sun.

Definition of a tithi:
- When the Sun and Moon are at the same longitude a new lunar month of 30 tithis begins.
- Each tithi is 12° of the Moon's lead over the Sun. The first tithi ends when the Moon is exactly 12° ahead, the second at 24°, the third at 36°, and so on: after n tithis the lead is (12 × n)°.
- After 30 tithis the lead is 360°: the Moon has caught up with the Sun and a new month begins.
- Tithi indicates the water element (Jala Tattva).
- Each tithi has a ruler, one of the eight planets from Sun to Rahu, following the order of the weekdays: Sun rules the 1st, Moon the 2nd, Mars the 3rd, Mercury the 4th, and so on.

Tithi calculation:
- Tithi = (Moon's longitude − Sun's longitude) ÷ 12°. If the Moon's longitude is less than the Sun's, add 360° to the Moon's longitude first.
- If the difference is less than 180° it is Shukla Paksha; if more than 180°, Krishna Paksha.
- The quotient is the number of tithis elapsed; the remainder is the part of the current tithi that has elapsed.

Tithi in the birth chart:
- Tithi is Jala Tattva, ruled by Venus, and relates to passion and desires.
- Check the birth tithi and its ruling planet. The placement of that planet in the birth chart indicates the type of relationships — strong, weak or rough — and the attitude towards relationships.

Uses of tithi (Muhurta): the tithi represents lunar energy, identified with mental energy, and should never be neglected.
1. The 4th, 8th, 12th and 14th tithis (Chaturthi, Ashtami, Dwadashi, Chaturdashi) are unsuitable for doing or starting any auspicious work.
2. The 4th, 6th, 8th and 12th tithis (Chaturthi, Shashthi, Ashtami, Dwadashi) are to be avoided for commencing any work or journey. The notes describe these as tithis "of Full Moon days", as printed.
3. Religious propitiation is necessary if birth falls in the last 48 minutes (four-fifths of an hour) of Panchami (5th), Dashami (10th), Amavasya (30th) or Purnima (15th).
4. Krishna Chaturdashi (14th tithi of the dark fortnight): the period is divided into six parts; the first sixth is auspicious and the rest inauspicious, requiring shanti (religious propitiation).
5. Siddha (successful) tithis: business or auspicious work begun when a tithi falls on its listed weekday results in success.

### 6.4 Karana
- A karana is half a tithi: every tithi has two karanas.
- Each karana is 6° of angular distance between the Sun and Moon, giving 60 karanas in a lunar month. Their duration varies.
- There are only 11 karanas, each with a planetary lord.
- Its placement indicates the nature of one's work and how one applies oneself in the world.
- The 7 movable karanas — Bava, Balava, Kaulava, Taitila, Garija, Vanija, Vishti — repeat 8 times, from the second half of Shukla Pratipada to the first half of Krishna Chaturdashi.
- The 4 fixed karanas occur once each: Kimstughna (first half of Shukla Pratipada), Shakuni (second half of Krishna Chaturdashi), Chatuspada (first half of Amavasya) and Naga (second half of Amavasya).

### 6.5 Yoga
- "Yoga" literally means union. The yoga at a given time is found by adding the longitudes of the Moon and the Sun.
- There are 27 yogas in the Panchanga, called Nitya ("eternal, constant") Yogas because they repeat perpetually in a fixed cycle, like the nakshatras.
- Unlike tithis and karanas, which vary in length and occurrence, a yoga is always operating. They are eternal determinants of auspiciousness in the daily Panchanga.
- The yoga in force at birth is the native's birth yoga.
- Calculation: (Sun's longitude + Moon's longitude) ÷ 13°20'. If the sum exceeds 360°, deduct 360° first.
- Placement shows how we are within the larger community, how we bring things together, our connection to the world at large, and our feelings of support and beneficence.
- Hard yogas — Vishkumbha, Atiganda, Shoola, Ganda, Vyaghata, Vajra, Parigha, Vaidhriti — show a lack of feeling supported or connected.

### 6.6 Worked example — tithi, karana and yoga

Chart: Moon 13°22' Gemini (73°22' = 73.3667°); Sun 0°56' Sagittarius (240°56' = 240.9333°). Other placements shown: Rahu 15°51' Pisces, Mars 14°34' Aries, Saturn (R) 16°49' Aquarius, Venus 6°23' Capricorn, Mercury 10°27' Scorpio, Jupiter 16°43' Libra, Ketu 15°51' Virgo.

Tithi:
1. The Moon's longitude is less than the Sun's, so add 360°: 73.3667° + 360° = 433.3667°.
2. 433.3667° − 240.9333° = 192.4334°.
3. 192.4334° ÷ 12° = 16.0361: 16 tithis have passed, so the 17th is running.
4. 17 − 15 = 2nd tithi of Krishna Paksha: Krishna Dwitiya.

Karana:
1. 192.4334° ÷ 6° = 32.0722: 32 karanas have passed, so the 33rd is running.
2. The 33rd karana is Taitila, the first half of Krishna Dwitiya.

Yoga:
1. 240.9333° + 73.3667° = 314.3000° (below 360°, so nothing is deducted).
2. 314.3000° ÷ 13°20' = 23.5725: 23 yogas have passed, so the 24th is running.
3. The 24th yoga is Shukla.

### 6.7 Inauspicious tithis, karanas, yogas and nakshatras at birth

The sages laid down general effects of tithis, karanas and yogas, which the notes omit as too general. Nakshatras at birth, however, have a marked influence on the native. Vara, tithi, nakshatra and yoga are all considered in Muhurta, the selection of an auspicious time for important acts and ceremonies.

- Tithis:
  - Propitiation is necessary for birth in the last 48 minutes (four-fifths of an hour) of Panchami, Dashami, Amavasya or Purnima.
  - Krishna Chaturdashi: of its six parts the first is auspicious; the rest are inauspicious and require shanti.
- Karana: birth in Bhadra Karana (another name for Vishti) requires propitiation.
- Yogas: birth in Ganda (printed as "Yamaganda" in one place), Vyatipata, Parigha, Shoola or Vaidhriti is inauspicious and requires the prescribed prayers.
- Nakshatras: Ashwini, Ashlesha, Magha, Jyeshtha, Moola, Revati. Particularly significant are:

| Nakshatra | Pada | Position of the Moon in its sign |
|---|---|---|
| Ashwini | 1st | 0°00'–3°20' Aries |
| Magha | 1st | 0°00'–3°20' Leo |
| Moola | 1st | 0°00'–3°20' Sagittarius |
| Ashlesha | 4th | 26°40'–30°00' Cancer |
| Jyeshtha | 4th | 26°40'–30°00' Scorpio |
| Revati | 4th | 26°40'–30°00' Pisces |

- It is customary among orthodox Hindus to perform shanti if a child is born with the Moon in any of these sectors.
- Propitiation is likewise made if a child is born in the nakshatra of either parent, a brother or a sister.

## 7. Muhurta

- A muhurta is a measure of time of 48 minutes, calculated from sunrise, used to determine the auspicious time for starting an event.
- For judging the quality of time it is not exactly 48 minutes every day: it varies with sunrise, sunset and local noon.
- There are 30 muhurtas in an Ahoratra (from one sunrise to the next): the first 15 belong to the day (sunrise to sunset) and the next 15 to the night (sunset to sunrise).
- Each quarter of the day holds 7½ muhurtas: sunrise to local noon, local noon to sunset, sunset to midnight, and midnight to the next sunrise.
- The four cardinal points of time — sunrise, local noon, sunset and midnight — are the four Gayatri Padas. The span between two of them, 7½ muhurtas, is a Prahara; four Praharas make one full day.
- Each muhurta is assigned to a nakshatra.
- The 8th muhurta, spanning the last half-muhurta of the morning and the first half-muhurta of the afternoon, is Abhijit Muhurta. It is highly auspicious, corresponding to Abhijit Nakshatra, ruled by Hari (Narayana).
- The 26th and 29th muhurtas are very auspicious.
